Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability)



The Petitioner, a biotechnology company, seeks to classify the Beneficiary, a radiochemist, as an alien
of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C.
§ l l 53(b)(1)(A). This first preference (EB-1) classification makes immigrant visas available to aliens
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim
and whose achievements have been recognized in their fields through extensive documentation.

Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that
the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before
us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS' decision
was arbitrary and capricious because SCOPS failed to follow statutory and case law and created its
own standard of proof that far exceeds what is legally sufficient. The Petitioner claims that he
submitted more than sufficient evidence to meet the burden ofproof to establish that he is an individual
of extraordinary ability.

The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

I. LAW

Section 203(b)(1) of the Act makes visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if:

(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through
extensive documentation,
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of
extraordinary ability, and
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively
the United States.

The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in that small percentage who have
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) ("EB-1
petitions, nicknamed Einstein visas, are coveted visas reserved for only the most talented noncitizens
with extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics"). The implementing
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate
sustained acclaim and recognition of the beneficiary's achievements in the field through a one-time
achievement, that is, a major, internationally recognized award. If that petitioner does not submit this
evidence, then they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the
ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items, such as awards, published
material in certain media, and scholarly articles). Additionally, if the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)
do not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence
to establish the beneficiary's eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4).

Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the beneficiary is among the small percentage
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010)
(discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

II. ANALYSIS

A. Evidentiary Criteria

internationally recognized award, the Petitioner must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS decided that the Beneficiary satisfied two criteria
relating to judging and scholarly articles but did not satisfy any of the four remaining claimed criteria
relating to lesser recognized awards, published material, original contributions, and a leading or
critical role. After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude that the Petitioner has not
demonstrated eligibility for this highly restrictive immigrant classification by a preponderance of the
evidence. While we may not address each piece of evidence individually, we have reviewed and
considered each one.

2. "Published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major
media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence
shall include the title, date, and author of the material, and any necessary translation." 8 C.F.R.
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii).

To meet this criterion, the Petitioner must first establish that the published material was related to the
Beneficiary and his specific work in the field for which classification is sought. See generally 6 USCIS
Policy Manual, supra, at F .2(B)(1). The published material should be about the person, relating to the
person's work in the field, not just about the person's employer and the employer's work or about
another organization and that organization's work. Id. However, the person and the person's work


3

need not be the only subject of the material; published material that covers a broader topic but includes
a substantial discussion of the person's work in the field and mentions the person in connection to the
work may be considered material about the person relating to the person's work. Id.

Second, the Petitioner must show that the publication qualifies as a professional publication, major
trade publication, or major media publication. Id. In evaluating whether a submitted publication is a
professional publication, major trade publication, or major media, relevant factors include the intended
audience for professional and major trade publications and the relative circulation, readership, or
viewership for major trade publications and other major media. Id.

The record includes an abstract co-authored by the Beneficiary and published in The Journal of
Nuclear Medicine in 2023. The Petitioner provided evidence that this abstract was presented at the
2023 SNMMI annual meeting, highlighted on the SNMMI website's news page, and included in the
SNMMI's online searchable database. The Petitioner did not sufficiently establish that the SNMMI
annual meeting, the SNMMI website, or the SNMMI' s online searchable database constitutes a
professional publication, major trade publication, or major media publication. See 8 C.F.R. §
204.5(h)(3)(iii).

The Petitioner also submitted internet articles published by Applied Radiation Oncology, Aunt Minnie,
Imaging Technology News, Medical Xpress, and Medical News Today. While these articles discuss a
newly discovered radionuclide-based agent and the theranostics potential of this agent in cancer
imaging and targeted drug delivery, these articles are not about the Beneficiary and do not mention
the Beneficiary's name in connection with the work. An article that is not about the Beneficiary does
not meet this regulatory criterion. See, e.g., Negro-Plumpe v. Okin, 2:07-CV-820-ECR-RJJ at *1, *7
(D. Nev. Sept. 8, 2008) (upholding a finding that articles about a show are not about the actor).
Moreover, the Petitioner did not include the author of most of these internet articles, as required by
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). Furthermore, The Petitioner did not submit independent, objective
evidence establishing that the websites are major media. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). See also
Braga v. Poulos, No. CV 06 5105 SJO (C. D. CA July 6, 2007) aff'd 2009 WL 604888 (9th Cir. 2009)
(concluding that self-serving assertions on the cover of a magazine as to the magazine's status is not
reliant evidence of major media). The evidence to establish that the publication qualifies as a
professional publication, major trade publication, or major media publication submitted should be
specific to the media format in which it was published. Because the articles were published online,
the evidence should relate to the websites.

4. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v).

To satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must demonstrate that the Beneficiary has made original
contributions in the field and that the original contributions are of major significance to the field. See
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(1). Evidence that the person's work was funded,
patented, or published, while potentially demonstrating the work's originality, will not necessarily
establish, on its own, that the work is of major significance to the field. Id. For example, published
research that has provoked widespread commentary on its importance from others working in the field,
and documentation that it has been highly cited relative to others' work in the field, may be probative
of the significance of the person's contributions to the field of endeavor. Id. Similarly, evidence that
the person developed a patented technology that has attracted significant attention or
commercialization may establish the significance of the person's original contribution to the field. Id.

Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the person's
contributions may provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed original contributions of major
significance, particularly when the record includes documentation corroborating the claimed
significance. Id. Submitted letters should specifically describe the person's contribution and its
significance to the field and should also set forth the basis of the writer's knowledge and expertise.
Id.

The Petitioner provided the Beneficiary's Google Scholar profile showing a list of his published
articles and citation record, copies of articles co-authored by the Beneficiary, lists of publications that
cite the Beneficiary's work, copies of sample articles that cite the Beneficiary's work, and an
assignment of an invention co-invented by the Beneficiary. While the Petitioner provided
documentation showing that the Beneficiary is listed as a co-inventor of an invention on a patent
application, the record lacks sufficient evidence that the invention has attracted significant attention
or commercialization. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(1). Additionally, while
the record includes copies of scholarly articles co-authored by the Beneficiary and his citation record,
the Petitioner has not sufficiently established that the number of independent cites per article for the
Beneficiary's published work is indicative of original scientific contributions of major significance in

The opinions of the Beneficiary's references are not without weight and have been duly considered.
We may, in our discretion, use as advisory opinions statements submitted as expert testimony. See
Matter of Caron International, 19 I&N Dec. 791, 795 (Comm'r 1988). However, we are ultimately
responsible for making the final determination regarding an alien's eligibility for the benefit sought.
Id. The submission of reference letters supporting the petition is not presumptive evidence of
eligibility; we may evaluate the content of those letters as to whether they support the alien's
eligibility. See id. at 795-796; see also Matter ofV-K-, 24 I&N Dec. 500, n.2 (BIA 2008) (noting that
expert opinion testimony does not purport to be evidence as to "fact"). Thus, the content of the
references' statements and how they became aware of the petitioner's reputation are important
considerations. Even when written by independent experts, letters solicited by an alien in support of
an immigration petition are of less weight than preexisting, independent evidence that one would
expect of a scientist who has made original contributions of major significance in the field. Cf
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F.Supp.3d 126, 134-35 (D.D.C. 2013) (concluding that our decision to give little
weight to uncorroborated assertions from professionals in the field was not arbitrary and capricious).

The support letters acknowledge the Beneficiary's scientific expertise in chemistry and provide details
on the Beneficiary's research in radiopharmaceutical development, molecular imaging, and
radiochemistry, his research results, and the impact of his research work in the field. The letters reflect


6

that the Beneficiary's research constitutes original contributions based. However, the letters do not
sufficiently demonstrate that his contributions are "of major significance in the field." See 8 C.F.R. §
204.5(h)(3)(v). The phrase "major significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning.
Silverman v. Eastrich Multiple Investor Fund, L.P., 51 F. 3d 28, 31 (3rd Cir. 1995) quoted in APWU
v. Potter, 343 F.3d 619, 626 (2nd Cir. 2003). To be considered a contribution of major significance
in the field of science, it can be expected that other experts would have reproduced or otherwise applied
the petitioner's results. Otherwise, the impact of the petitioner's work is difficult to gauge. Cf
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F.Supp.3d 126, 134-35 (D.D.C. 2013) (upholding a finding that a ballroom dancer
had not met this criterion because she did not demonstrate her impact in the field as a whole).

While the Beneficiary's research is original, the record lacks sufficient evidence showing that other
scientists or radiochemists are implementing or currently applying the Beneficiary's research findings,
so as to establish that these findings have already impacted the field "in a significant manner." While
the record shows that the Beneficiary co-authored scholarly articles detailing his research findings and
these articles were published in scientific journals, publications and presentations are not sufficient
evidence under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) absent evidence that they were "of major significance."
Kazarian v. USCIS, 580 F.3d 1030, 1036 (9th Cir. 2009) aff'd in part 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010).
In 2010, the Kazarian court reaffirmed its holding that we did not abuse our discretion in finding that
the alien had not demonstrated contributions of major significance. 596 F.3d at 1122. Without
sufficient documentation corroborating the claimed significance asserted in the letters, the record does
not sufficiently establish the Beneficiary's original scientific contributions of major significance in the
field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). Therefore, the Petitioner has not sufficiently demonstrated that
the Beneficiary meets this criterion.

Although the Petitioner argues that SCOPS' decision was arbitrary and capricious because SCOPS
created its own standard of proof that far exceeds what is legally sufficient, SCOPS has acknowledged
and analyzed various documents on record by a preponderance of the evidence but concluded overall
that the quality of the evidence lacked probative value in supporting the Beneficiary's eligibility for
the EB-1 classification. Under the preponderance standard, we consider not only the quantity, but also
the quality of the evidence, including its relevance, probative value, and credibility. Matter of
Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376; see also Matter of E-M-, 20 I&N Dec. 77, 79-80 (Comm'r 1989).
When SCOPS provides a reasoned consideration to the petition and has made adequate findings, it
will not be required to specifically address each claim the petitioner makes, nor is it necessary for it to
address every piece of evidence the petitioner presents. Guaman-Loja v. Holder, 707 F.3d 119, 123
(1st Cir. 2013) (citing Martinez v. INS, 970 F.2d 973, 976 (1st Cir. 1992); see also Kazemzadeh v. US.
Atty. Gen., 577 F.3d 1341, 1351 (11th Cir. 2009); Casalena v. US. INS, 984 F.2d 105, 107 (4th Cir.
1993). Here, the record reflects that SCOPS provided a reasoned consideration to the petition and had
made adequate findings.

B. Final Merits Determination

The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a major one-time achievement
or documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we do not need to provide the type
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding it does not support a finding that the
Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought.

from denying an extension of the original visa based on a reassessment of petitioner's qualifications).

III. CONCLUSION

The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top
of their respective fields, rather than for those progressing toward that goal. USCIS has long held that
even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary
ability" standard. Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Here, the Petitioner
has not sufficiently shown the significance of the Beneficiary's work is indicative of the required
sustained national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in
the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section
203(b)(1)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not sufficiently demonstrate that the Beneficiary
has garnered national or international acclaim in the field and that he is one of the small percentage
who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(1)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R.
§ 204.5(h)(2).

For the reasons we have discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated by a preponderance of
the evidence the Beneficiary's eligibility for the EB-1 classification. The appeal will be dismissed for
the above-stated reasons, with each considered as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.

ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
