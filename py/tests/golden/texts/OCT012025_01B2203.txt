Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision

Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability)



The Petitioner, a data scientist, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See section
203(b)(1)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(1)(A). This first
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate their
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id.

SCOPS denied the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the
Petitioner had not established she had a major, internationally recognized award, or that she met at
least three of the ten regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R.
§ 103.3.

The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

I. LAW

Section 203(b)(1)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens of extraordinary ability if:
they have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been
demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; they seek to enter the United States to
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and their entry will substantially benefit the United
States prospectively. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).

The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis of the evidence
to determine whether petitioners have sustained national or international acclaim and whether their
achievements have been recognized in the field. First, a petitioner can provide evidence of either "a

major, international recognized award," or satisfaction of at least three of ten evidentiary criteria at
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1

Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration
Services (USCIS) then considers the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination
and assesses whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates
that the individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor.
See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the
documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the
context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011); see generally 6 USCIS
Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (providing, as guidance, USCIS' two-step
extraordinary ability analysis).

II. ANALYSIS

The Director determined the Petitioner had not established that she received a major, internationally
recognized award and explained that she must therefore satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed she satisfied the regulatory criteria at
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (v), (viii), and (ix). SCOPS detennined the Petitioner only established
the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). On the Form I-290B, Notice of Appeal or Motion,
the Petitioner lists the basis ofher appeal and claims she will file a brief. As of the date ofthis decision,
we have not received a brief.

On the Form I-290B, the Petitioner argues that SCOPS "failed completely to take into consideration
the extraordinary nature of [the P]etitioner's status as a women of color in the field of information
technology" and that her "peer group to be considered was 'women of color in information
technology. " ' The Petitioner does not provide any legal authority in support of this argument that we
should consider her eligibility within a subgroup of the population. The requirements of establishing
extraordinary ability are in section 203(b)(1)(A) of the Act and relevant guiding regulations. We lack
the authority to waive or disregard any of the Act' s requirements, as implemented by regulation. See
United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683, 695(1974) ("So long as this regulation is extant it has the force
oflaw.")

However, we withdraw SCOPS' finding that the Petitioner commanded a high salary or other
significantly high remuneration for services in relation to others in the field under
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). In order to meet 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix), petitioners must demonstrate
that their salary or remuneration is high relative to the compensation paid to others working in the
field. The Petitioner asserts in the record below that her current hourly wage as a consultant for one
company is $60 per hour and for another is $65 per hour. The Petitioner submitted 2023 salary
information for data scientists, which has $51.93 as the national estimate for the hourly rate, $70.99
as the hourly rate for those paid at the 75th percentile and $88.51 for those paid at the 90th percentile.
She also includes location specific salary for Florida, which is averaged at $51.20 hourly. While we
consider geographical compensation surveys, the Petitioner's employment contracts have her
employers located in California and New Jersey. According to the record, her services are contracted
out to other companies, but the Petitioner does not explain whether location affects her pay rate.
Moreover, while we acknowledge the Petitioner is paid more than the national average, she is not paid
at a rate of those in the 75th or 90th percentile ofher field. She further argues that her combined salary
is $125 per hour. However, the comparative salary data measures an hourly rate for one job, not
combined jobs. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(1) (providing as guidance
factors to consider in compacting data, including rate being measured). Based on the record, the
Petitioner has not established her salary or remuneration is high relative to the compensation paid to
others working in the field, and that she has met this criterion.

Further, although the Petitioner argued in the record below that she satisfied the criterion at
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii), SCOPS did not analyze whether the Petitioner demonstrated she
performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished
reputation to satisfy the criterion. However, this oversight is not outcome determinative, as the
analysis of this criterion would not have led to a final merits determination. Moreover, the Petitioner
has not demonstrated her eligibility for this criterion. The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii)
requires petitioners to first demonstrate that they have performed in a leading or critical role for an
organization, establishment, or a division or department of an organization or establishment. A
supporting role may be considered critical if the petitioner's performance in the role is (or was)
important. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(1) (providing, as guidance,
examples ofleading or critical roles and considerations for evaluating the evidence). In the underlying
record, the Petitioner described her duties as a contract employee for the companies she works for,
claiming for instance that for one company she established robust processes, provided strategic
insights, and delivered essential reports. However, she did not explain or corroborate her statements
with references to the record as to how these processes or insights or reports were critical to the
company or established her role as leading. She also claimed that she played a leading or critical role
as a mentor for the [REDACTED] by, for example, providing strategic guidance to
her mentees. The letters in the record from her mentees speak to her supportiveness and assistance

The criterion then requires a petitioner to establish that the organization or establishment, or the

B. Summary of Evidentiary Criteria

The Petitioner has not established that she has received a major, internationally recognized award
under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), or that she satisfies at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). As a result, we need not provide the type of final merits determination
referenced in Kazarian , 596 F.3d at 1119-20. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25(1976) (stating
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the
ultimate decision); see also Matter ofL-A-C-, 26 I&N Dec. 516, 526 n.7 (BIA 2015) (declining to
reach alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). Nevertheless, we advise
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not support a conclusion
that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought.

The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top
of their respective fields, rather than for individuals progressing toward the top. See 8 C.F.R.
§ 204.5(h)(2) (explaining "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that small percentage who
have risen to the very top of the field ofendeavor"). USCIS has long held that even athletes performing
at the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter of
Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Here, the record as a whole does not demonstrate
a level of recognition that indicates the required sustained national or international acclaim or
demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No.
101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(1)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not
otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very top
of the field of endeavor.

III. CONCLUSION

The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability.

ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
