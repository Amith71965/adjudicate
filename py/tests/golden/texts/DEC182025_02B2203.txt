Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability)



The Petitioner seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability in the field of innovation ecosystem
development. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C.
§ l 153(b)(1)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation.

Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not satisfy at least
three, as required, of the initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R.
§ 103.3.

The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

I. LAW

Section 203(b)(1)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if:

(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through
extensive documentation,

(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of
extraordinary ability, and

(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively
the United States.

The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are
"reserved for only the most talented aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major,
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material
in certain media, and scholarly articles).

Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d
1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then,
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination);
see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US. Citizenship &
Immigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), ajf'd, 683 F.3d 1030 (9th Cir. 2012).

II. ANALYSIS

Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). SCOPS determined the Petitioner met only two (published materials under 8 C.F.R.
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii) and judging under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv)) of the six claimed categories of
evidence. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he fulfills four additional criteria. Although the record
supports SCOPS' favorable decision for the judging criterion, we do not concur with SCOPS'
published material determination and will withdraw it, discussed below.

A. Memberships

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields."
The Petitioner argues his eligibility based on membership with the Forbes Financial Council (FFC)
and the Alchemist Accelerator (AA).

Regarding FFC, the Petitioner provided screenshots from forbes.com indicating that "[s]enior-level
financial executives of companies earning $1 M USD in revenue annually, or have received $1 M USD
in funding are eligible to apply for membership." In addition, the Petitioner submitted a letter from
R-P-, co-founder and COO of FFC, who stated that "[t]o be considered, one must be senior-level
financial services executive or leader with a successful company that: 1. [h]as at least $1M in annual
revenue and/or 2. [h]as at least $1M in financing" and "an internal review process is done by our
professional selection committee to determine the level of their outstanding qualifications within the

USCIS determines if the association for which the person claims membership requires that members
have outstanding achievements in the field as judged by recognized experts in that field. 1 FFC requires
individuals to be a senior-level financial services executive or leader, and the companies to have $1M

III. CONCLUSION

The Petitioner did not establish eligibility for any of the categories of evidence discussed above.
Although the Petitioner also claims qualification for the role criterion under 8 C.F.R.
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii), we need not reach this ground because the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial
evidentiary requirement of at least three under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). See Kinuthia v. Eiden, 702 F.
Supp. 3d 5, 11 (D. Mass. 2023), aff'd sub nom. Kinuthia v. Trump, No. 23-2029, 2025 WL 3491324
(1st Cir. Aug. 12, 2025) (declining to consider a criterion when the plaintiff cannot satisfy at least
three). Accordingly, we decline to reach and hereby reserve these issues. See INS v. Bagamasbad,
429 U.S. 24, 25(1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision).

Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1)(A)(i) of the Act.
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R.
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen,
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa.").
Therefore, we also decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter.

Regardless, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already
at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter of Price, 20
I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even major league level athletes do not
automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary
ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (stating that extraordinary ability is
