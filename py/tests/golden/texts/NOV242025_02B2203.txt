Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability)



The Petitioner, a legal consultant, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b0(1)(A). This first
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation.

SCOPS denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner qualifies as an
alien of extraordinary ability either as the recipient of a major, internationally recognized award, or
that the Petitioner meets at least three of the ten initial regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. §
204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3.

The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter a/Christa 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

I. LAW

Section 203(b)(1)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if:

1. the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which
has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation,

11. the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability,
and

m. the alien' s entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United
States.

The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in "that small percentage who have risen
to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation

at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence,
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material
in certain media, and scholarly articles).

Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the
very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing
a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number
of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F.
Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

A. Evidentiary Criteria

Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. §
204.5(h)(3)(ii)(i)-(x). In the initial petition, the Petitioner claimed to satisfy four criteria. SCOPS
evaluated the claims the Petitioner made in the initial petition and in response to the request for
evidence (RFE) and concluded the Petitioner only satisfied one criteria, participation as a judge of
others' work at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). On appeal, the Petitioner claims the same four criteria she
discussed in her petition and in response to SCOPS' RFE. After reviewing all the evidence in the
record, we conclude the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility for this highly restrictive immigrant
classification for the reasons we will discuss below.

Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in thefieldfor which class[fication
is sought, which require outstanding achievements oftheir members, as judged by recognized
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii).

In order to satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must show that the association for which she claims
present or past membership requires that members have outstanding achievements in the field, as
judged by recognized experts in that field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1),
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Examples of associations in the field requiring outstanding
achievements of their members may include a membership in certain professional associations and a
fellow with certain organizations or institutions. Id.

Associations may have multiple levels of membership, and the level of membership afforded to the
person must show that in order to obtain that level of membership, recognized national or international
experts judged the person as having attained outstanding achievements in the field for which
classification is sought. Id. Relevant factors that may lead to a conclusion that the person's
membership in the association was not based on outstanding achievements in the field include
instances where the person's membership was based solely on (1) a level of education or years of
experience in a particular field, (2) the payment of a fee or by subscribing to an association's
publications, and (3) a requirement for employment in certain occupations, such as union membership
or guild affiliation for actors. Id.

The Petitioner claims that she meets this criterion based on membership in the American Bar
Association (ABA); I I Bar I l and the Florida Association for Women Lawyers
1
(FA WL). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for documentation of the alien's
membership in associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding
achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their
disciplines or fields. As noted by SCOPS in the decision, the Petition provided documentation of her
membership in these associations but did not provide any evidence to establish that her membership
in these associations require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields.

On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that the FAWL and DCBA are "significant state-level organizations."
She contends her membership in the ABA and I I "confirms her professional rigor." She also
maintains that "ABA membership is reserved for individuals who meet bar admission and ethical
standing requirement." But the evidence in the record does not adequately establish if admission "as
a member of the association" is contingent upon the Petitioner having attained outstanding
achievement in her field as determined by recognized experts in the field. The Petitioner has not
submitted sufficient evidence demonstrating the requirements for membership in the organizations.
While we acknowledge that membership into these associations may be important for professional
development, the evidence is not sufficient to satisfy this criterion. It is the Petitioner's burden to
submit evidence that sufficiently corroborates her claims. Statements made without supporting
documentation are of limited probative value and are insufficient to satisfy the Petitioner's burden of
proof Mitter o[Soffici, 22 I&N Dec. 158, 165 (Comm'r 1998). Here, the Petitioner's membership in
the ABA, and FAWL is not sufficient to support fulfillment of this criterion.

Evidence of the alien's authorship ofscholarly articles in the field, in professional or major
trade publications or other major media. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi).

USCIS first determines whether the person has authored scholarly articles in the field. In academia, a
scholarly article reports on original research, experimentation, or philosophical discourse, is generally
peer reviewed, and may be published in professional or scientific journals or conference presentations.
In other fields, a scholarly article should be written for others having profound knowledge in the field.
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, at F.2(B)(1).

Second, USCIS determines whether the publication qualifies as a professional or major trade
publication or other major media, we consider such relevant factors as the circulation or readership
relative to other media in the field and the intended audience (for professional journals). Id.

As evidence for this criterion, the Petitioner references the ublication of her article titled, .I..._ ___.
._______________________________. which she stated, addressed
"substantive issues in tax law reform and corporate governance." The Petitioner claimed that it was a
scholarly article published in JusBrasil and that JusBrasil is "an established platform with a readership
of over 1.6 million monthly users and 900,000 legal professionals." SCOPS determined that the
Petitioner did not meet the requirements of this criterion. Specifically, SCOPS noted that the audience
of the published material may be the general public and not for learned individuals. On appeal, the
Petitioner contends that the article "directly addresses the topic of professional legal relevance and
was disseminated through a widely respected medium in the legal profession." The Petitioner farther
contends that JusBrasil is a major media and a "well-established professional forum."

As defined in the academic arena, a scholarly article reports on original research, experimentation, or
philosophical discourse. Id. It is written by a researcher or expert in the field who is often affiliated
with a college, university, or research institution. Id. Scholarly articles are generally peer reviewed
by other experts in the field of specialization. Id. In general, it should have footnotes, endnotes, or a
bibliography, and may include graphs, charts, videos, or pictures as illustrations of the concepts
expressed in the article. Id.

Here, the record does not show that the Petitioner's article contains the characteristics of scholarly
articles, as considered in the academic arena. The Petitioner did not establish that she wrote the article
as a researcher or expert. Furthermore, the Petitioner's article does not contain features of academic
scholarly articles, such as footnotes or bibliographies. For these reasons, the Petitioner did not show
that her article qualifies as a scholarly article within the purview of academia.

For other fields, a scholarly article should be written for learned persons in that field. Id. Learned
persons include all persons having profound knowledge of a field. Id. Here, the Petitioner did not
show that her article was written for learned persons in the legal field or for other particular learned
persons. Moreover, the Petitioner did not demonstrate that JusBrasil is intended for learned persons
rather than a general audience or readers who subscribe to the online publications. Finally, the
Petitioner did not show that JusBrasil qualifies as major media. See Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp.
3d 168, 185 (D.N.J. 2021) (finding argument that internet-accessible outlets by their nature have a
worldwide reach substantially undermines any reasonable interpretation of "major").

While the Petitioner provided some information about the standing and readership of JusBrasil, the
about page of JusBrasil states that they are "a startup that unites [la]w and [te ]chnology to make justice
go beyond the borders of the courts and reach the homes of any citizen, empowering their decisions
through information." Therefore, the Petitioner has not offered sufficient evidence to support her
claims that she published scholarly articles in her field in professional or major trade publications or
other major media, and that her article was written for learned persons in her field.

For all these reasons, the Petitioner did not demonstrate she fulfills this criterion.

B. Reserved Issues

We note the Petitioner claims she met an additional two criteria related to her participation as a judge
of the work of others in the same or an allied field of specification for which classification is sought
and evidence that she has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments
that have a distinguished reputation. However, as noted above, the Petitioner's unsuccessful
demonstration of her eligibility under the criteria we discuss means she cannot numerically meet the
required number of at least three criteria out of the 10 available and the four she claimed. Accordingly,
we need not decide on SCOPS' favorable conclusions for the fourth criterion as well as the Petitioner's
claimed eligibility for the eighth criterion. We, therefore, reserve these issues. See INS v.
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25(1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision).

C. Final Merits Determination

The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we do not need to provide the type
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding it does not support a finding that the
Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought.

III. CONCLUSION

Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1)(A)(i) of the Act.
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R.
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen,
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa.").

For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of
extraordinary ability. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for
the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here.

ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
