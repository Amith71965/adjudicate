Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision

Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability)



The Petitioner, an electrical engineer in the "semiconductor power electronics engineering" field,
requests classification under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category
as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section
203(b)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(1)(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this
category must demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document
recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b)(1)(A)(i) of the Act.

Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met
two of ten alternative evidentiary criteria - one less than needed for a final merits determination. On
appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS relied on evidence supporting his prior denied EB-1
petition and did not fully review the evidence with this petition. He argues that he satisfied four
additional evidentiary criteria.

The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010).
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO
2015), we conclude that he met at least three alternative evidentiary criteria. But, because he has not
established his receipt of enough acclaim and recognition to place himself among the small percentage
at his field's very top, we will dismiss the appeal.

I. LAW

To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they:

• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident.

Section 203(b)(1)(A)(i-iii) of the Act.

The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).

Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three
of ten alternative evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary
standard and the requirements at section 203(b)(1)(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and
Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits determination. To merit final approval,
the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim"
and recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See
Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (implementing a two-step extraordinary
ability analysis); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

II. ANALYSIS

A. The Petitioner

The record shows that, in his home country of Nigeria, the Petitioner received a bachelor of
engineering degree in electrical/electronic engineering and a master's degree in business
administration. He also has a U.S. master of science degree in electrical and computer engineering
and has published research articles in the semiconductor power electronics field. The Petitioner has
more than 15 years of employment experience in the energy and manufacturing industries. He worked
in Africa as a project lead engineer for one company, advancing wire line logging operations on a rig
site, and as a technical sales and product manager for three other firms, handling industrial automation
and instrumentation/control projects.

The Petitioner currently works for a U.S. energy company as a systems test continuous engineer. In
this role, he develops new energy optimization and control devices. He stated: "My work is
motivating new energy frontiers and innovations with the industrialization and digitalization of energy
manufacturing processes that will lead to economic empowerment of US businesses, national security,
contribute to medical progress, improved logistics, and economic growth."

B. The Alternative Evidentiary Criteria

The record does not demonstrate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major international
award. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). He must therefore satisfy at least three of the ten alternative
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x).

The record supports SCOPS' findings that the Petitioner submitted evidence of his:

• Authorship of scholarly articles in his field; and
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration for his services, in relation
to others in this field.

to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4).

See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi), (ix). Upon review, we find that he met at least one additional alternative
evidentiary criterion: judging others' work in his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv).

To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[ e]vidence of the[ir] participation, either
individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field of specification
for which classification is sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). "The petitioner must show that [they
have] not only been invited to judge the work of others, but also that [they] actually participated in the
judging of the work of others in the same or allied field of specialization." 6 USCIS Policy Manual
F.2(8)(1), Criterion 4.

SCOPS found that the technical project reviews the Petitioner completed for companies did not satisfy
this evidentiary criterion. But SCOPS did not discuss evidence that he reviewed others' research
articles in his field. He submitted copies of letters and email messages asking him to review multiple
manuscripts for a quarterly scientific and industrial journal and thanking him for his services after
their completion. Examples ofjudging others' work under this criterion may include "[p Jeer reviewing
for a scholarly journal, as evidenced by a request from the journal to the person to do the review,
accompanied by proof that the review was actually completed." 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1),
Criterion 4. Thus, the Petitioner met this evidentiary requirement.

The Petitioner has satisfied the requisite minimum number of alternative evidentiary criteria. See
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). We therefore need not reach and hereby reserve consideration of whether he
met the other evidentiary requirements he claims to have satisfied. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S.
24, 25(1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues
unnecessary to their ultimate decisions).

USCIS must now conduct a final merits determination on the petition. SCOPS did not make such a
finding in this case. We will therefore conduct the final merits determination ourselves.

C. The Final Merits Determination

To merit final approval as an alien with extraordinary ability, a petitioner must demonstrate - through
evidence of sustained national or international acclaim and recognition of their achievements in their
field- that they are one of the small percentage who has risen to their field's very top. 6 USCIS Policy
Manual F.2(8)(2). They "must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it
demonstrates that the person has achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition
in their field of expertise." We review evidence in the totality and must consider "any potentially
relevant evidence in the record, even if such evidence does not fit one of the ... regulatory criteria or
was not presented as comparable evidence." Id.

On appeal, the Petitioner claims that "[h ]is contributions have been vital to the energy industry." He
notes that a former employer issued him a 2018 "~---,----,---------,-~ ' for developing a system that
monitors critical energy equipment. The system uses wireless transmitter sensors to measure vibration
depth and temperature to detect mechanical faults and defects in rotating pumps, heat exchangers,
turbines, and other vibratory equipment. An area director with the former employer stated:
"Previously, companies would often wait until the critical assets equipment failed and then have to
scramble to make repairs losing valuable time." The area director said his company was the first to


3

deploy the system worldwide and continues to use and sell it. The system includes software allowing
customers to monitor their equipment in "real-time." The area director stated that the monitoring
system:

had a significant impact to the field because it helps prevent unexpected plant
shutdowns and failures for customers in the oil and gas, food and beverage, chemical,
life sciences, power, water and wastewater industries. [The Petitioner]'s original
monitoring solution led to an 85% increase in the [company's] reliability services
business revenue with the award of several lifecycle services and maintenance
contracts, operational excellence achievements status, and improved machinery health
monitoring systems.

The area director stated: "Other companies in the energy industry have started using [the Petitioner's]
vibration monitoring solutions to reduce carbon emissions, mitigate climate change and create
environmental sustainability initiatives."

A managing director of a Nigerian energy service company stated that his firm has also used the
Petitioner's solutions in various multi-million-dollar projects and operations. He stated: "[The
Petitioner]'s technical value propositions and product solutions led to the upgrade of legacy systems
at [a large energy company's] prolific energy installations in offshore West Africa, resulting in
improved performance of critical assets, and enhanced operational excellence." He stated that other
large energy companies, together comprising about 12% of the global industry, have also adopted the
Petitioner's solutions in critical projects. He stated:

The fact that they have adopted [the Petitioner]'s solutions means that his original
solutions have had a major impact on the field as a whole. It also indicates that his
innovations are not just theoretically sound but also practically viable and beneficial in
real-world scenarios.

The reported use of the Petitioner's solutions by major energy companies is impressive. But the
evidence falls short of placing him among the small percentage at his field's very top. His former
employer issued him the 2018 I ~ and the record does not demonstrate the award' s
availability to others in the field. See Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 WL
1566071, at *4 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (affirming that internal awards from an employer did not
"translate[] to sustained national or international acclaim"). Also, none of the major energy companies
that reportedly adopted the Petitioner's solutions provided letters on his behalf. Rather, the letters he
submitted are from his current or former employers, clients, or others who personally know him. "It
is generally expected that one whose accomplishments have garnered sustained national or
international acclaim would have received recognition for their accomplishments well beyond the
circle of their personal and professional acquaintances." 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3).

As additional evidence of his claimed extraordinary ability in his field, the Petitioner cites critical roles
he has purportedly played for energy companies. For example, a manager at the Petitioner's current
employer described the Petitioner as "one of the few people that have the skills and experience of
providing original solutions and making improvements in both industrial and residential energy
settings." But the record does not explain whether the Petitioner is one of the few people with such


4

capabilities in his field or merely at his employer. See Joseph v. Dir. of Texas Serv. Ctr., USCIS, No.
24-40249, 2 WL 458001, at *4 (5th Cir. Feb. 11, 2025) (affirming USCIS' finding that a petitioner's
leading role in a successful or distinguished company does not demonstrate that "the impact of his
work has risen to the level where it places him at the very top of the field").

Similarly, the record shows the Petitioner's authorship of published scholarly articles in his field. But
he has not demonstrated that his papers have generated a high number of citations in his field. See
Agrawal v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *23 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025) (affirming
USCIS' finding that evidence of a petitioner's scholarly articles "does not demonstrate that the
research reported in those articles has made an impact on the field sufficient to provoke widespread
commentary").

Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability. Proposed Rule for
Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). Congress indicated that
this immigrant visa category was meant "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the very
top of their field of endeavor." Id. The Petitioner met the category's initial evidentiary requirements
and demonstrated his receipt of acclaim and recognition in his field. But, considering the evidence as
a whole, he has not established his claimed place among the small percentage of his field's very top.
See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 386 (5th Cir. 2022) ("[G]iven the lofty bar for extraordinary
ability classifications, we cannot say that the agency acted arbitrarily when it determined that [a
petitioner] was not 'extraordinary' but merely very good."); Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954
(Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (holding that even athletes performing in their sport's premier league do not
automatically meet the extraordinary ability standards).

III. CONCLUSION

The Petitioner has met the requisite amount of alternative evidentiary criteria. But he not demonstrated
that he is among the small percentage at his field's very top. We will therefore affirm the petition's
denial.

ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
