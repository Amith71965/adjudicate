The Petitioner, an educational technologist, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary
ability in the field of education. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(1)(A),
8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(1)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim
and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation.

SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish, as required, that the Petitioner
met three of the initial ten evidentiary criteria for this classification listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3).
The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3.

The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

I. LAW

Section 203(b)(1)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if:

(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through
extensive documentation,

(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work m the area of
extraordinary ability, and

(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the
United States.

The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major,
internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R.
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not
readily apply to the individual's occupation.

Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010)
(discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that we
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and
within the context of the totality of the evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably
true." Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376.

4. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v).

To evaluate whether a person satisfies this criterion, we first determine whether the person has made
original contributions in the field. We then determine whether these original contributions are of major
significance in the field. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(1).

The Petitioner claims eligibility for this criterion based upon her doctoral dissertation, published
scholarly articles, book chapters that she co-authored, and presentations, all of which generally focus
on teaching English as a second language. In support ofthis contention, the Petitioner provided copies
of her research articles, co-authored book chapters, her curriculum vitae, recommendation letters, as
well as documentation of her citation record below. The scholarly articles, book chapters, and
presentations indicate the original nature of the Petitioner's contributions. However, the Petitioner
has not demonstrated that these contributions are of major significance in the field.

In the recommendation letters below, the authors generally praise the Petitioner's research and indicate
its originality. Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the
person's contribution may provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed original contributions
of major significance, particularly when the record includes documentation corroborating the claimed
significance. Id. Here, the letters do not offer detailed explanations of the nature and significance of
this research to provide the context allowing us to evaluate her claimed contribution of major
significance. For example, the letters do not explain the unusual influence or high impact her research
or work has had on the overall field. See Jafarov v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No.
23 CIV. 3372(PAE), 2024 WL 69056, *8 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) (finding the petitioner's testimonial
letter mostly consisted of conclusory statements about the significance of his work and his potential).

B. Final Merits Determination

Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1)(A)(i) of the
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need not
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v.
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25(1976) (per curiam) (holding
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the
ultimate decision).
