Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision

Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability)



The Petitioner is a data management platform specializing in streaming that seeks to classify the
Beneficiary as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section
203(b)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(1)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas
available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive
documentation.

SCOPS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record
did not establish that the Beneficiary had a major, internationally recognized award, nor did it
demonstrate the Beneficiary warranted a favorable decision within the final merits determination. The
matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § I 03 .3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to
demonstrate eligibility to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) by a preponderance of
the evidence. Section 291 of the Act; Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). We
review the questions in this matter de novo . Matter of Christo 's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO
2015). Upon de novo review, we will sustain the appeal.

I. LAW

To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate:

• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and
• The alien ' s entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future.

Section 203(b)(1)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).

The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that

meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles).

Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115,
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then,
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination);
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,394 (5th Cir. 2022).

II. ANALYSIS

A. Evidentiary Criteria

Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that the Beneficiary has received a major,
internationally recognized award, it must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied five of the criteria relating
to: published material, original contributions, authorship of scholarly articles, perf01ming in a leading
or critical role, and commanding a high salary or remuneration, but that it had not satisfied the criteria
associated with prizes or awards. However, SCOPS further decided the Petitioner did not demonstrate
the Beneficiary qualified for this immigrant visa within a final merits determination examining the
totality of it evidence and claims. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that it has made such a showing
in the final merits dete1mination and after reviewing all the evidence in the record, we agree that the
highly restrictive nature of this immigrant classification has been satisfied.

B. Final Merits Determination

As the Petitioner has submitted the requisite initial evidence we will evaluate whether it has
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, the Beneficiary's sustained national or
international acclaim, that he is one of the small percentage at the top of the field of endeavor, and that
his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits
determination, we analyze the Petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to
determine if the Beneficiary's successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability
in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(1)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20). See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2),
https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual (stating that USCIS officers should then evaluate the evidence
together when considering the petition in its entirety to determine if the petitioner has established by
a preponderance of the evidence the required high level of expertise of the immigrant classification).
In this matter, we determine that the Petitioner has not shown the Beneficiary is qualified and is eligible
for this classification.

We begin noting that we do not agree with the Petitioner's statement that once he met at least three of the
regulatory criteria, this "creates a rebuttable presumption: the beneficiary meets the legal standard for
eligibility and shifts the burden of proof to then obligate USCIS to raise material challenges to
specifically-identified requisite facts, or approve this petition." This may be how the Petitioner views or


2

considers the process to operate, but that is not the case and it did not offer any legal authority to support
this statement. Once at least three criteria are satisfied, it remains the Petitioner's burden to also
demonstrate to USCIS that a favorable decision is warranted when considering all of the evidence and
claims collectively. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 388 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding that after a
petitioner satisfies at least three criteria, "the agency then conducts a 'final merits determination' to
determine whether, as a whole, the evidence is sufficient to demonstrate that the applicant meets the
'required high level of expertise"'). That same court stated "[t]he regulation does not say that meeting
the regulatory criteria presumptively qualifies an applicant for a visa . . . . And the final merits
determination speaks to the statute's requirement of 'extraordinary ability which has been demonstrated
by sustained national or international acclaim."' Amin, 24 F.4th at 391-92. This passage also illustrates
the burden remains on a petitioner to satisfy the stated requirements in a final merits determination. So,
there is no burden-shifting exercise onto USCIS at any point in this adjudicative process.

While the burden does not shift to USCIS, the agency is required to engage with the evidence and claims
and evaluate those against the appropriate standards. The statute mandates that a filing patiy has the
burden of proving eligibility for an immigration benefit. Section 291 of the Act. Further, the
regulations are clear that the filing party bears the burden ofproving eligibility by submitting evidence
for consideration and adjudication. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(8)(i)-(iii). While an adjudicator must
detennine whether the evidence indicates that the patiy in question qualifies, that dete1mination is an
evidentiary one that does not stem from any burden on USCIS. Cf Matter of Negusie, 28 I&N Dec.
120, 154 (A.G. 2020). Rather, USCIS' responsibility is to folly consider the evidence for relevance,
probative value, and credibility, both individually and within the context of the totality of the evidence,
and to provide a reasoned decision of whether that material satisfies the filing party's burden of
proof. Matter of Chmvathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. We will perf01m that analysis here.

The Beneficiary co-founded the petitioning organization, a data management platform, and serves in
his current role as its chief executive officer. Since establishing the data management platform, he has
overseen its evolution from a developer tool into an enterprise-grade ecosystem and secured tens of
millions of dollars in investor funding to aid its expansion in the United States.

SCOPS discussed many of the relevant areas to consider when they broadly mentioned the Beneficiary's
achievements and concluded each area was not representative of one who is at the top of their field of
endeavor. It appears that SCOPS did not evaluate the Beneficiary's achievements collectively, or in their
totality, as is required. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(2). SCOPS also granted
the contributions of major significance criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), but in the final merits stated
he "and his company['s] contributions appear to be successful, however, the evidence does not indicate
that [they] have had a 'major significance' on his field of endeavor." That statement runs contrary to the
criterion's requirements in part one of this adjudication.

While the appeal brief and response to the notice of intent to deny could have fashioned a better organized
argument of how the Petitioner has demonstrated the Beneficiary qualifies under the final merits
determination, a review of the entire record leads us to conclude that the petitioning organization has met
its burden in these proceedings. The Petitioner illustrates the transformative and unique nature of the
services the Beneficiary has built, implemented, and overseen at the petitioning organization. Those
efforts touch on several of the regulatory criteria that SCOPS granted in step one of their adjudication.
Were we to only evaluate the Beneficiary's accomplishments separately aligning with each area covered


3

by the criteria, we too would not consider placing him among those warranting an approval under this
immigrant classification. But just as a single strand of silk or wool appears plain and unspectacular on its
own in a tapestry, individual achievements may seem common within a field of endeavor. However,
when the tapestry's structural strands are intersected with the carefully woven colorful weft, they form a
rich and unique pattern. The same can be said about the collective evidence in this record ofproceeding.

The Beneficiary enabled the petitioning organization to simplify the use of a popular data streaming
platform that is used by more than 80 percent of Fortune 100 companies. He shepherded and streamlined
this platform that acts as a high-speed digital "postal system" of sorts for data that, instead of statically
resting in a database, continuously flows in a complex stream of events. The Beneficiary's efforts have
effectively lowered the barrier to entry for real-time data streaming by replacing complex coding and
manual processes with a simplified interface that is useful to more working groups than only specialized
data engineers. He has helped establish the petitioning company as a significant presence and a leading
management layer for those using the data streaming platform.

The evidence in the record sets the Petitioner apart from others through a "career of acclaimed work
in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also
section 203(b)(1)(A) of the Act. The petition in its entirety demonstrates that the Beneficiary possesses
a level of expe1iise consistent with a finding that he is one of a small percentage at the very top of the
field of endeavor and that he has sustained national acclaim in his field. See section 203(b)(1)(A) of
the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. See also Matter of Price, 20 T&N
Dec. 953,956 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994).

III. CONCLUSION

The Petitioner has established that the Beneficiary meets at least three of the evidentiary criteria listed
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). It has also demonstrated he enjoys sustained national and
international acclaim and that his achievements have been recognized through extensive
documentation. Lastly, the Petitioner has shown that the Beneficiary intends to continue working in
his area of expertise and that he will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. He therefore
qualifies for classification as an individual of extraordinary ability.

ORDER: The appeal is sustained.
