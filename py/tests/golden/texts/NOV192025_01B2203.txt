The Petitioner, an automotive technician and repairer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C. §
l 153(b0(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation.

SCOPS denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner qualifies as an
alien of extraordinary ability either as the recipient of a major, internationally recognized award, or
that the Petitioner meets at least three of the ten initial regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. §
204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3.

The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter a/Christa 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

I. LAW

Section 203(b)(1)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if:

1. the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which
has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation,

11. the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability,
and

m. the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United
States.

The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in "that small percentage who have risen
to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation

at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence,
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material
in certain media, and scholarly articles).

Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the
very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing
a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number
of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F.
Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

II. ANALYSIS

The Petitioner asserts that he has demonstrated eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability as
evidenced by the prestigious awards he has received and the valuable contributions and
accomplishments he has made in the field of auto body design and restoration. He intends to continue
his work in auto body repair, restoration, and reconstruction.

A. Evidentiary Criteria

Because the Petitioner has not indicated or demonstrated that he has received a major, internationally
recognized award, he must instead satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R.
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii)(i)-(x). In his initial petition, the Petitioner claimed to meet five criteria. SCOPS
evaluated the claims the Petitioner made in the initial petition and in response to the request for
evidence (RFE) and concluded the Petitioner only satisfied one criterion, participation as a judge of
others' work at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). On appeal, the Petitioner reasserts the same five criteria
presented in the initial filing and adds one additional criterion that he claimed in response to SCOPS'
RFE. He contends that he satisfies "at least five of the ten regulatory criteria." After reviewing all
the evidence in the record, we conclude the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility for this highly
restrictive immigrant classification for the reasons we will discuss below.

2. Evidence of Membership in Associations Requiring Outstanding Achievements of Members

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's membership in
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields."
In order to satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must show that the association for which he claims
present or past membership requires that members have outstanding achievements in the field, as
judged by recognized experts in that field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1),
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Examples of associations in the field requiring outstanding
achievements of their members may include a membership in certain professional associations and a
fellow with certain organizations or institutions. Id.



3

Associations may have multiple levels of membership, and the level of membership afforded to the
person must show that in order to obtain that level of membership, recognized national or international
experts judged the person as having attained outstanding achievements in the field for which
classification is sought. Id. Relevant factors that may lead to a conclusion that the person's
membership in the association was not based on outstanding achievements in the field include
instances where the person's membership was based solely on (1) a level of education or years of
experience in a particular field, (2) the payment of a fee or by subscribing to an association's
publications, and (3) a requirement for employment in certain occupations, such as union membership
or guild affiliation for actors. Id.

The Petitioner submitted evidence of his membership in the Brazilian association of industry,
commerce, and services for excellence in automotive repairs (ABRAESA). The Petitioner states that
ABRAESA is a "respected professional association that plays a crucial role in maintaining high
standards within the automotive repair industry." In support of his claim, the Petitioner provided
copies of the organizations' bylaws, code of ethics, and mission statement for ABRAESA and
Sindifupi, a sister organization to ABRAESA, according to the Petitioner. SCOPS, however, found
that the evidence was insufficient to demonstrate that either organization requires outstanding
achievement as a condition of membership or that members are evaluated by recognized experts in the
field. SCOPS also concluded that the bylaws submitted do not indicate that membership is limited to
individuals who have attained outstanding achievements.

On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS "erroneously dismissed" his membership as not
"meeting regulatory criteria." He argues that his membership "was not the result of a general
application but rather a direct invitation from ABRAESA." The Petitioner maintains that his invitation
to join the organization is "clear evidence of [his] professional distinction." However, the record does
not establish that admission by invitation is contingent upon outstanding achievements in the field as
determined by recognized experts in that same field. Furthermore, the documents provided do not
demonstrate that ABRAESA limits membership to individuals who have attained outstanding
achievement in the field, nor do the bylaws reflect such a requirement.

While we acknowledge that membership of these associations may be important for professional
development, the evidence is not sufficient to satisfy this criterion. It is the Petitioner's burden to
submit evidence that sufficiently corroborates his claims. Statements made without supporting
documentation are of limited probative value and are insufficient to satisfy the Petitioner's burden of
proof Matter of Sojfici, 22 I&N Dec. 158, 165 (Comm'r 1998). Here, the Petitioner's membership in
the ABRAESA is not sufficient to support fulfillment of this criterion.

5. Display of Work at Artistic Exhibitions or Showcases

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) requires "[e]vidence of the display of the alien's work in
the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." In addition to demonstrating that the displayed work
was the alien's work product, the venues (virtual or otherwise) where the work was displayed must be
artistic exhibitions or showcases. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(1),
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. USCIS may consider non-artistic exhibitions or showcases as
part of a properly supported claim of comparable evidence under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). Id. While
Merriam-Webster's online dictionary definition of "exhibition" includes public showings other than
those that are artistic in nature, the plain language of the criterion includes the modifier "artistic" and
explicitly requires that the exhibitions or showcases be artistic in nature. Id.

6. High Salary or Other Significantly High Remuneration

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements.
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field.
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(1), www.uscis.gov/policy-rnanual.

The Petitioner submitted a personal declaration attesting that he earned 60,000 Brazilian Real (BRL)
in both 2022 and 2023, along with a February 14, 2025, pay stub showing an hourly rate of $32 from
I ILLC. He also provided a salary survey for a mechanic technician from salary.corn and
auto body technician, a general earning range for car tinkerers, and a service contract with I [REDACTED]
I ILLC. The Petitioner asserts that his documented salary of 60,000 BRL exceeds the
average salary of 30,000 BRL for an auto body restoration technician in Brazil. He further claims that
his U.S. hourly wage of $32 is significantly higher than the $19.36 per hour average for comparable
positions in this field. SCOPS determined that the criterion had not been met because the Petitioner's
evidence did not establish that he has commanded a high salary or other significantly high
remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field. SCOPS further stated that the Petitioner
has to present that his salary places him at the very top of his field. On appeal, the Petitioner states
that he has submitted sufficient evidence to demonstrate that he is making "well above the typical
range for professionals" in his field.

Although the Petitioner submitted documentation relating to his compensation for 2022 and 2023 and
asserted that his hourly rate exceeds the reported average, the evidence does not demonstrate that his
income places him among the small percentage of individuals at the very top of his field. He did not
demonstrate, for example, how his earnings compare to others at the very top of the field or other
renowned mechanic and auto body technicians or car repairers. The Petitioner did not submit
sufficient documents to demonstrate that he has received notoriety or attention based on his profits or
salary separating himself from others in the field or placing him in the upper echelon. See Bodhankar
v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5



7

(finding that the plaintiff's high salary was not so high as to indicate that he has risen to the very top
of the field). Moreover, the Petitioner did not provide comparative data, industry reports, or
independent evidence showing how his compensation is exceptional relative to his peers. Average
and median salaries are insufficient to establish the Petitioner commanded a high salary in relation to
others in the field. Kinuthia, 702 F. Supp. 3d at 11; Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F. Supp. 3d 837, 853
(C.D. Cal. 2022). Proving a salary is "above average" typically doesn't suffice to establish a "high
salary" or "significantly high remuneration." More comprehensive wage data is needed to effectively
demonstrate high earnings relative to peers.

Accordingly, the Petitioner did not show that he meets this criterion.

B. Final Merits Determination

The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we do not need to provide the type
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. See INS v. Bagamasbad,
429 U.S. 24, 25(1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision).

III. CONCLUSION

Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1)(A)(i) of the
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v.
Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa.").

For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of
extraordinary ability. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for
the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here.
