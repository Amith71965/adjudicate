Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability)



The Petitioner, a wealth management strategist and financial consultant, seeks classification as an alien
of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C.
§ 1153(b)(1)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation.

Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record showed that the
Petitioner met the initial evidence requirement for the classification by meeting at least three of the
evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), but did not establish her sustained national or
international acclaim or that she is one of the very small percentage at the top of her field. The matter
is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3 .

The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

I. LAW

To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they:

• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States.

Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the
field. Section 203(b)(1) of the Act.

The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3).

Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria,
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4 th 383,
394 (5 th . Cir. 2022).

II. ANALYSIS

The Petitioner holds the U.S. equivalent of master's degrees in management and economics, and has
been employed various roles in the financial services sector for many years. She states that she intends
to work as a financial consultant in the United States through creating her own consultancy business.

A. Evidentiary Criteria

Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she received a major, internationally
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met three of the evidentiary
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), relating to published material about her, her participation as a
judge of the work of others, and a high salary in relation to others in her field. On appeal, the Petitioner
asserts that she also meets four additional criteria, as well as the overall requirements for classification
as an alien of extraordinary ability. 1 After reviewing all of the evidence in the record, we will
withdraw SCOPS' determination regarding all three of the criteria they found that the Petitioner meets.
In addition to the Petitioner not meeting the requisite three evidentiary criteria, we conclude that she
has not established that she has sustained national or international acclaim and is one of the small
percentage at the very top of her field of endeavor.

6. Commanded a High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration in Relation to Others in the
Field

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) seeks "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." Evidence
that an alien's compensation is high relative to that of others in the field may include comparative
salary data such as geographical and position-appropriate compensation surveys. See generally 6
USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(1).

In support of this criterion, the Petitioner submitted a document titled "salary certificate" from
I I showing that the Petitioner earned a net income ofKZT 15,752,961 over six months
in 2024 (more than KZT 2.6 million per month) in the role of "Main division, Business Director."
Other documents included a statement from a bank regarding the balance of an account held by the
Petitioner, a partially translated document showing deposits into the Petitioner's pension account, and
an undated and partially translated article regarding salaries in Kazakhstan from the website
finprom.kz. Based on this evidence, the Petitioner asserted that her salary was 67.4% higher than the
national average monthly salary for managers in Kazakhstan, which she states is KZT 1,568,200.

However, the source of the Petitioner's comparative figure is not apparent because the article from
finprom.kz is only partially translated. Any document in a foreign language must be accompanied by
a foll English language translation. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(3). The translator must certify that the English
language translation is complete and accurate, and that they are competent to translate from the foreign
language into English. Id. While the text of the first two pages of the article is in English, the notes
and titles to the graphical information on those pages are in a foreign language. And those pages are
followed by three additional pages that are also in a foreign language and not accompanied by a
translation to English. Because the Petitioner did not submit a properly certified English language
translation of this complete article as required, we accord it no weight as we cannot determine whether
it supports their claim.

In addition, even if the Petitioner's assertions regarding her comparison to the average monthly salary
for managers in Kazakhstan were accurate, they would not be sufficient to show that her remuneration
was high in comparison to others in her field of wealth management and financial consulting. The
only source of information in the record regarding her employment and duties with this firm is her
own statements and resume, where she indicates that the company was involved in "brokerage
consulting and investment project facilitation." The record provides no basis for her comparison to
"managers" in Kazakhstan as being appropriate to determine whether her remuneration was high
compared to others in her field, as there is no indication beyond her own statements that she performed
the duties of a manager while employed by [REDACTED]
Further, the portion of the article from finprom.kz which is in English shows a wide disparity in the
average salary for a manager based upon industry and location, as emphasized by the statement that
"not every industry and not every region can boast of a salary of 788. 7 thousand tenge." As noted
above, a salary survey should reflect compensation that is geographically and occupationally­
appropriate for comparison to a petitioner's compensation. Here, because the relevant portion of this
survey is not translated into English, we cannot determine whether the figure used by the Petitioner as
a basis for comparison uses geographically and occupationally-appropriate data.


8

Finally, the figure used by the Petitioner states that the figure she used for comparison to her salary is
an average of the monthly salary for managers in Kazakhstan. Even if we were to consider the data
from this insufficiently translated salary survey, this limited information is not sufficient to establish
that the Petitioner's earnings were high compared to that of others in her field. See Strategati, LLC
v. Sessions, No. 3:18-CV-01200-H-AGS, 2019 WL 2330181, at *7 (S.D. Cal. May 31, 2019)
(upholding evidence of average salary levels is an insufficient basis for comparison to show high salary
in relation to others in the field).

Based upon the foregoing analysis, we disagree with SCOPS' conclusion that the Petitioner meets this
criterion and withdraw that conclusion.

Per the forgoing analysis, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that she has met six of the seven
evidentiary criteria that he has claimed to meet. While the Petitioner challenges SCOPS' conclusion
that she also does not meet the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), relating to her original
contributions of major significance to the field, she cannot satisfy the threshold "initial evidence"
requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) of meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria. Therefore,
we decline to reach and hereby reserve her arguments on appeal related to that criterion. See INS v.
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25(1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision).

B. Final Merits Determination

The Petitioner has not met the initial evidence requirements for the requested classification, and is
therefore not eligible for classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. However, because SCOPS
concluded that the Petitioner did not establish her sustained national or international acclaim or that
she was one of the small percentage at the very top of her field, we will conduct a final merits analysis
below.

In a final merits determination, we analyze an individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of
the evidence to determine if their achievements are sufficient to establish sustained national or
international acclaim and that they are one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of
endeavor. See section 203(b)(1)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596
F.3d at 1119-20. Here, the Petitioner has not offered sufficient evidence that she meets that standard.

The record shows that the Petitioner is a successful bank executive and personal financial consultant.
The central pillar of her claim as an alien of extraordinary ability is her development of what she
claims to be a proprietary personal financial consulting and wealth management methodology, the
360° Portfolio Methodology, which she indicates integrates legal, behavioral, tax, and
intergenerational wealth planning into a single strategy. Several of the reference letters she submitted
state that she either trained others in this methodology, which was then implemented within client
organizations, or applied it in serving individual clients.

For instance, G-Y-, who indicates that she is the head of private banking for a bank in Kazakhstan,
states that the Petitioner delivered a training session on sales strategies for high net worth individuals
to the bank's sales team in 2022 which included the introduction of her 360° Portfolio Methodology.

The record also does not support wider acclaim for the Petitioner's work via other indicators. As
previously discussed, much of the published material in which she has been named and pictured

organizations in a consulting or business development role more recently. While these roles may have
been important ones for the individuals and institutions that she served, the record does not show that
they brought the Petitioner acclaim at the national or international level or placed her among those at
the top of her field. For instance, the evidence of her earnings covers only a six-month period when
she was employed by a company she states that she founded, and the record does not include
appropriate comparable evidence that those earnings for even that brief period would have placed her
amongst the highest earners in her field. Further, the record does not indicate that she was recognized
by her employers, clients, collaborators, or peers in other significant ways. We note that the letter of
recognition and certificate from [REDACTED] were not mentioned in any other reference
letters, published materials, or elsewhere in the record, indicating that they were not recognized to any
degree beyond the issuing organization.

The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's
eligibility for the benefit sought. Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary
ability by requiring the petitioner demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and
present "extensive documentation" to show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field.
Section 203(b)(1)(A)(i) of the Act. This highly restrictive visa classification is intended for individuals
already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter of
Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994)(concluding that even major league level athletes
do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary
ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2002) (stating that extraordinary ability is
such an elite level of accomplishment). Considering the full measure of the Petitioner's career and
achievements in her field, she has not established that the significance of her work is indicative of the
required national or international acclaim or is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in her field.
And she has not demonstrated that her achievements place her amongst the small percentage at the top
of her field.

III. CONCLUSION

Because we conclude that the Petitioner does not meet the initial evidence requirements for this visa
classification, we withdraw SCOPS' determination to the contrary. In addition, we conclude that the
Petitioner has not established the requisite level of acclaim and standing in her field to show eligibility
as an alien of extraordinary ability.
