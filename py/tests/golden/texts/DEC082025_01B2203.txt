Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability)



The Petitioner, an entrepreneur, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(1)(A). This first
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation.

Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish
that the Petitioner met the initial evidence requirements for this classification either through evidence
of a one-time achievement (a major, internationally recognized award) or meeting at least 3 of the I 0
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8
C.F.R. § 103.3.

The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

I. LAW

To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they:

• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States.

Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the
field. Section 203(b)(1) of the Act.

The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3).

Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria,
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4 th 383,
394 (5 th . Cir. 2022).

II. ANALYSIS

The Petitioner is a co-owner of a bakery near I lFlorida that specializes in Brazilian cakes and
sweets. She states that she is an owner of two other businesses based in Florida, and previously served
as an owner and purchasing director for an auto parts distributor in Brazil. She intends to continue
working as a chief executive or business administrator in the United States.

A. Evidentiary Criteria

Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she received a major, internationally
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS found that the Petitioner did not meet any of the 6 evidentiary
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) that she claimed. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that she
meets the same 6 evidentiary criteria:

• (i) Lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards
• (ii) Membership in associations requiring outstanding achievements
• (iv) Participation as a judge of the work of others in the field
• (v) Original contributions of major significance in the field
• (viii) Leading or critical role for organizations with a distinguished reputation
• (ix) High salary in relation to others in the field

After reviewing all of the evidence in the record, we conclude that the Petitioner does not meet the
initial evidence requirement and is not eligible for classification as an alien of extraordinary ability.

2. Evidence of Membership in Associations Requiring Outstanding Achievements of Members

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields."

The Petitioner submitted evidence of her membership in the National Society of Leadership and
Success (NSLS) and the American Culinary Federation (ACF). In their decision, SCOPS concluded
that the record did not show that either of these organizations requires outstanding achievements of
their members. On appeal, the Petitioner's counsel asserts that NSLS membership is "extended only
to individuals who meet strict eligibility criteria, including academic excellence and demonstrable
leadership potential." 3 But counsel's unsubstantiated assertions do not constitute evidence. See, e.g.,
Matter ofS-M-, 22 I&N Dec. 49, 51 (BIA 1998) ("statements in a brief, motion, or Notice of Appeal
are not evidence and thus are not entitled to any evidentiary weight"). And even if counsel's assertions
were accurate and substantiated, the Petitioner has not shown that academic excellence and leadership
potential are outstanding achievements in the field of business administration, or that those
achievements are judged by recognized national or international experts in their fields.

A letter from the President and CEO of NSLS states the following regarding the association's
membership requirements:

In order to achieve induction in the NSLS, [the Petitioner] committed to attending
leadership training focused on collaborative teamwork, goal setting, high achievement,
leadership skills and styles, self motivation, and accountability.

3. Participation as a Judge of the Work of Others in the Field

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) calls for "[e]vidence of the alien's participation, either
individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field of
specialization for which classification is sought." Here, the Petitioner asserts that she meets this
criterion based on her judging of a culinary competition hosted b y ~ - - - - - - - ~ An email
correspondence chain verifies that she was asked to judge the work of local high school students in
this competition, and later thanks her for her participation. As with our discussion pertaining to the
awards criterion above, we note that the Petitioner is relying on evidence relating to the field of
culinary arts, while her stated field of expertise is business administration. She does not explain, nor
does the record demonstrate, how evaluating the cooking of high school students equates to judging
the work of other business administrators, executives or entrepreneurs. For this reason, we conclude
that the Petitioner has not established that she meets this criterion.

4. Commanded a High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration in Relation to Others in the
Field

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) seeks "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." SCOPS
acknowledged the Petitioner's jointly-filed IRS Form 1040 tax return for 2018, as well as what appears
to be financial documentation in a foreign language that was not accompanied by a foll English
translation. 4 But SCOPS' decision noted that the Petitioner did not submit additional information in
response to the RFE to show that her salary was high in relation to others in her field.

On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that her tax return reflects remuneration "that is substantially above
median income for culinary professionals, particularly when compared to the U.S. Bureau of Labor
Statistics wage data and I ~area benchmarks for small food business owners." This assertion
makes an inappropriate comparison and is not supported by the evidence. First, since the tax return
was jointly filed with the Petitioner's spouse and is not accompanied by either a Form W-2 or 1099 or
a Schedule C, Profit or Loss from Business, it is not possible to discern the Petitioner's salary or other
remuneration from this evidence. Second, the return shows wages and salaries of $3,866 in Line 1,
and total adjusted gross income of $36,218 on Line 7. However, this last figure includes income from
Schedule 1, which reports "foreign income" of $32,352. The record does not farther explain the source
of this income or whether it is attributable to the Petitioner or her spouse. And even if it is attributable
to the Petitioner, this evidence does not indicate that it reflects her remuneration as an entrepreneur,
business administrator, or executive.

B. Final Merits Determination

The Petitioner also challenges SCOPS' decision regarding the criteria at 8 C.F.R § 204.5(h)(3)(v) and
(viii), relating to her claims of having made original contributions of major significance to her field,
and that she played a leading or critical role for organizations having a distinguished reputation. But
because we have determined that the Petitioner does not meet four of the six criteria she asserts that
she meets on appeal, she cannot satisfy the initial evidence requirement of meeting at least three of the
evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). We therefore reserve the issue of whether she meets
the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) and (viii). See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25(1976)
(per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that
are unnecessary to the ultimate decision).

Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1)(A)(i) of the Act.
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold initial evidence requirements at 8 C.F.R. §
204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not farther
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen,
784 F.Supp.3d 798,814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient,
evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). Nevertheless, we
advise that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, and conclude that it does not support a
finding that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification
sought.
