The Petitioner, a producer of performing arts events, seeks classification as an individual of
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C.
§ l 153(b)(1)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation.

Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish
that the Petitioner met the initial evidence requirement for this classification through evidence ofeither
a one-time achievement or that she meets at least three of the alternative ten evidentiary criteria. The
matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3.

The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

I. LAW

To establish eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability, a petitioner must establish that he or she:

• Has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;
• Seeks to enter the United States to continue work in his or her area of extraordinary ability;
and that
• His or her entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States.

Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the
field. Section 203(b)(1) of the Act. The implementing regulation further states that the term
"extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very
top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner can demonstrate that he or she meets the initial evidence
requirements for this immigrant visa classification through evidence of a one-time achievement (that

is, a major, internationally recognized award). If such evidence is unavailable, then he or she must
alternatively provide evidence that he or she meets at least three of the ten listed criteria, which call
for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published material about them in qualifying
media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§
204.5(h)(2),(3).

Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria,
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4 th 383,
394 (5 th . Cir. 2022).

Upon de novo review, we conclude the Petitioner has not provided sufficient evidence to
establish her articles are scholarly as required by the plain language of the regulation. The
record does not show that the articles were reviewed by experts in the field, prior to publication,
or otherwise written for others with profound knowledge in the field. The Petitioner's evidence

Upon de novo review, we agree with SCOPS for the following reasons. USCIS determines whether
an alien's salary or remuneration is high relative to the compensation paid to others working in the
field. See generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(1) (Criterion 9). In evaluating the
sufficiency of evidence under this criterion, USCIS may consider geographical or position-appropriate
compensation surveys and organizational justifications to pay the compensation data. Furthermore,
for artists that are not paid an hourly rate but instead are paid a daily rate (which may not be equivalent
to 8 hours) or are paid a certain amount for a project (involving an unknown number of hours), the
alien may submit documentation regarding the number of hours worked on the project to show that,
as compared to others in their field, the alien commanded a significantly high renumeration. Such
documentation can include, but is not limited to, pay statements, personnel records, or testimonial
evidence from the relevant employer. Id.

C. The Petitioner's O-lB Visa

We acknowledge the Petitioner is the Beneficiary of an approved O-lB nonimmigrant visa petition.
Although USCIS has approved an O-lB nonimmigrant visa petition filed on behalf of the Petitioner,
this prior approval does not preclude USCIS from denying an immigrant visa petition which is
adjudicated based on a different statute, regulations, and case law. The nonimmigrant and immigrant
categories have different criteria, definitions and standards for persons working in the arts.
"Extraordinary ability in the field of arts" in the nonimmigrant O-lB category means distinction.
8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(ii). But in the immigrant context, "extraordinary ability" reflects that the alien
is among the small percentage at the very top of the field. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).

III. CONCLUSION

Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring a petitioner
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1)(A)(i) of the
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v.
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25.

ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
